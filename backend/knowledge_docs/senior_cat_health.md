# Senior Cat Health

## Overview

Cats are generally considered "senior" from around 10-11 years old, and "geriatric"
beyond about 15, though individual aging varies. Several chronic conditions become
substantially more common in this life stage, and - because cats are so good at
masking illness - they often present as subtle, gradual changes rather than a
single obvious symptom. This is a large part of why patterns that might be
dismissed in a younger cat (occasional vomiting, slightly reduced appetite, mild
weight change) are worth taking more seriously in an older cat.

## Common Conditions in Senior Cats

- **Chronic kidney disease (CKD)** - one of the most common conditions in older
  cats; early signs are often subtle (increased thirst/urination, gradual weight
  loss) before becoming more obvious.
- **Hyperthyroidism** - an overactive thyroid gland, often causing weight loss
  despite a normal or increased appetite, restlessness, and sometimes vomiting -
  this is part of why a chronic vomiting pattern in an older cat (see the
  "Vomiting" reference doc) is taken more seriously than an isolated hairball
  episode.
- **Arthritis/joint disease** - very common but easy to miss, since cats hide
  reduced mobility well; signs include reluctance to jump, stiffness after resting,
  or reduced grooming (a hunched, less flexible cat can't reach as well).
- **Diabetes** - increased thirst/urination and weight loss despite normal or
  increased appetite are common early signs.
- **Cognitive changes** - some older cats show disorientation, changes in
  sleep-wake patterns, or increased vocalization, particularly at night.

## Symptoms Worth Extra Attention in Senior Cats

- Any weight loss, even gradual, especially if appetite seems normal or increased
- Increased thirst or urination
- New reluctance to jump or climb
- Changes in litter box habits
- A vomiting or appetite pattern that would be "watch and wait" in a younger cat

## Common Questions Owners Ask

- "My older cat is losing weight but still eating well - should I worry?" - Yes,
  this combination (weight loss with normal/increased appetite) is a classic
  pattern for hyperthyroidism or diabetes and is worth a vet visit rather than
  being read as a good sign.
- "Is it normal for my senior cat to sleep more?" - Some increase in rest is
  typical with age, but a marked change in activity, especially combined with
  reduced grooming or reluctance to jump, is worth mentioning at a checkup.
- "How often should senior cats see the vet?" - Many vets recommend more frequent
  wellness visits (e.g. twice yearly rather than annually) for senior cats
  specifically because early-stage chronic disease is often only detectable
  through routine bloodwork before symptoms become obvious.

## When to See a Vet

Most senior-cat conditions described here progress gradually and are "schedule a
visit" rather than same-day emergencies on their own. Escalate to urgent care if a
senior cat shows any of the acute red-flag symptoms covered elsewhere in this
knowledge base (breathing difficulty, collapse, inability to urinate, etc.) - age
does not reduce the urgency of those, if anything senior cats have less reserve to
compensate.
