# Diarrhoea in Cats

## Overview

Diarrhoea (loose or watery stool) in cats is commonly caused by dietary indiscretion,
a sudden food change, intestinal parasites, or mild infection, and often resolves on
its own within a day or two with supportive care. Kittens are a special-risk group:
their small body size means they dehydrate far faster than adult cats, so the same
duration of diarrhoea that would be low-concern in an adult cat is treated more
urgently in a kitten.

## Symptoms

- Loose, watery, or unformed stool
- Increased frequency of bowel movements
- Straining before or during defecation
- Mucus or blood in the stool
- Black, tarry stool (a sign of digested blood higher in the GI tract)
- Accompanying vomiting, lethargy, or reduced appetite

## Questions to Ask

- How old is the cat - are they a kitten under 6 months?
- Any blood, black/tarry color, or unusual smell to the stool?
- Any vomiting alongside the diarrhoea?
- Are they still eating, drinking, and behaving normally otherwise?
- Any recent food change, new treats, or access to something they shouldn't have eaten?
- How long has this been going on?

## Red Flags

- Blood in the stool
- Black, tarry stool
- Severe lethargy
- Vomiting alongside diarrhoea
- A kitten under 6 months old
- Signs of dehydration (skin tenting, sunken eyes, dry gums)
- Diarrhoea combined with not eating

## Typical Triage Level

**Soon**, sooner for kittens or if any red flags are present. A single loose stool in
an adult cat that's otherwise well can usually be monitored for a day.

## Owner Guidance

Offer a bland, easily digestible diet in small portions and make sure fresh water
stays available; monitor stool for the next day. See a vet rather than continuing to
wait if the cat is a kitten, if there's any blood or black stool, if vomiting is also
present, or if the diarrhoea persists past 48 hours - kittens in particular can
deteriorate from dehydration much faster than the "wait a day or two" guidance that
applies to healthy adult cats.
