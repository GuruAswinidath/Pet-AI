# Urinary Obstruction in Cats (Feline Lower Urinary Tract Disease)

## Overview

Urinary obstruction is one of the most time-critical emergencies in cats. It happens
when the urethra - the tube that carries urine out of the bladder - becomes partially
or fully blocked, usually by a plug of crystals, mucus, and inflammatory debris, or
less commonly a true stone. Male cats are affected far more often than females because
their urethra is longer and narrower. A fully blocked cat cannot pass urine at all;
untreated, this leads to a backed-up, rupturing bladder and fatal kidney failure and
electrolyte disturbances, typically within 24-48 hours of the blockage starting.

## Symptoms

- Frequent, unproductive trips to the litter box
- Straining or squatting to urinate with little or no urine produced
- Crying out or vocalizing while in the litter box
- Excessive licking at the genital area
- A firm, painful, swollen bladder (a vet or experienced owner may feel this on the belly)
- Blood-tinged urine
- As it progresses: lethargy, vomiting, loss of appetite, and collapse

## Questions to Ask

- Is the cat male or female? (Male cats are at much higher risk.)
- When was the last time you actually saw urine produced, not just an attempt?
- Are they going in and out of the litter box repeatedly?
- Are they crying or seem to be in pain while trying to go?
- Any vomiting, lethargy, or loss of appetite alongside this?
- Has this happened before? (Cats who have blocked once are more likely to block again.)

## Red Flags

- Straining with little to no urine output
- Vocalizing or crying in the litter box
- A hard, painful abdomen/bladder
- Lethargy or vomiting on top of urinary straining
- Any male cat showing these signs, given the higher obstruction risk

## Typical Triage Level

**Emergency.** A cat suspected of being unable to urinate needs to be seen the same
day, ideally within hours - this is not a symptom that should be monitored at home
under any circumstances.

## Owner Guidance

Go to a vet or emergency clinic immediately if a cat is straining with no urine
output. Do not wait to "see if it resolves" - the window between first symptoms and
a life-threatening crisis can be less than two days. While traveling to the vet,
keep the cat calm and confined; do not attempt to express the bladder manually, as
a fully obstructed bladder can rupture under pressure. After treatment, ask the vet
about diet and environmental changes (litter box hygiene, water intake, stress
reduction) that reduce the risk of a repeat episode, since recurrence is common.
