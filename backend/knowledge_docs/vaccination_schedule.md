# Cat Vaccination Basics

## Overview

Vaccines are grouped into "core" (recommended for essentially all cats regardless
of lifestyle) and "non-core" (recommended based on individual risk factors like
outdoor access or multi-cat household exposure). Exact schedules vary by region,
local disease prevalence, and a vet's assessment of individual risk, so this is
general orientation rather than a substitute for a vet's specific recommendation.

## Core Vaccines

- **FVRCP** (a combination vaccine, commonly covering feline viral
  rhinotracheitis, calicivirus, and panleukopenia) - protects against common,
  highly contagious respiratory and GI viruses. Given as a kitten series with
  boosters, then periodically through adulthood.
- **Rabies** - required by law in many regions given its public health
  significance; schedule and frequency depend on local regulation and the specific
  vaccine product used.

## Non-Core Vaccines (Risk-Based)

- **Feline leukemia virus (FeLV)** - generally recommended for kittens and for
  adult cats with any outdoor access or contact with cats of unknown FeLV status,
  since transmission is through close contact (grooming, shared bowls, bite
  wounds).
- Other non-core vaccines may be recommended in specific circumstances (e.g.
  boarding, high-exposure environments) - this varies enough by situation that a
  vet's individual assessment matters more than a general rule here.

## Typical Adult Booster Frequency

After the kitten series, many core vaccines are boostered on an annual or
multi-year schedule depending on the specific vaccine and local guidelines - this
is one of the more region- and product-dependent parts of preventive care, so
"how often" is best confirmed at a wellness visit rather than assumed.

## Common Questions Owners Ask

- "My cat is strictly indoors - do they still need vaccines?" - Core vaccines are
  still generally recommended even for indoor-only cats, since some of these
  viruses are hardy enough to be carried in on clothing/shoes, and indoor cats can
  still have accidental outdoor exposure.
- "Can vaccines cause side effects?" - Mild, short-lived soreness at the injection
  site or brief lethargy is the most commonly reported reaction; a vet visit is
  warranted for facial swelling, vomiting, or any reaction that looks like an
  acute allergic response following a vaccine.
- "What's the difference between core and non-core?" - Core vaccines address
  diseases nearly all cats are at some risk for; non-core vaccines are added based
  on an individual cat's specific exposure risk.

## When to See a Vet

This is routine preventive care territory, not urgent triage. The one exception:
any cat showing facial swelling, hives, vomiting, or breathing changes shortly
after a vaccination should be evaluated the same day, since that pattern can
indicate an acute vaccine reaction.
