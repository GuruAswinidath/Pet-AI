# Breathing Difficulty in Cats

## Overview

Breathing difficulty in cats is treated as an emergency far more readily than in
dogs, for a specific physiological reason: cats do not pant as a normal cooling or
excitement behavior the way dogs do. Open-mouth breathing in a cat - outside of a
brief moment after intense exertion or extreme heat - is almost always a sign of
significant respiratory or cardiac distress. Cats are also skilled at masking
breathing difficulty until it becomes severe, so by the time an owner notices a
problem, the underlying condition (fluid in or around the lungs, asthma, heart
disease, airway obstruction, or trauma) is often already advanced.

## Symptoms

- Breathing with the mouth open
- Rapid, shallow breathing, especially at rest
- Labored breathing - visible effort with each breath
- Crouched posture with elbows pointed outward, neck extended (a posture cats adopt
  to maximize airflow)
- Gums or tongue that look blue, grey, or pale rather than pink
- Reluctance to move, hiding, or collapse after minimal exertion
- Audible wheezing, gasping, or choking sounds

## Questions to Ask

- Is the cat breathing with their mouth open?
- Are they hiding, reluctant to move, or sitting in an unusual crouched posture?
- Can you estimate their breathing rate at rest (breaths per minute)?
- What color are the gums - pink, pale, blue, or grey?
- Any recent trauma (fall, being hit by a vehicle) or a known heart condition?

## Red Flags

- Any open-mouth breathing in a cat
- Labored or rapid breathing at rest
- Blue, grey, or pale gums
- Gasping or choking
- Collapse, even briefly, after light activity
- A crouched, elbows-out breathing posture

## Typical Triage Level

**Emergency**, essentially always. This is one of the few symptoms in this knowledge
base with almost no "wait and see" home-monitoring pathway.

## Owner Guidance

Any open-mouth breathing, or breathing that looks labored or unusually fast while
the cat is at rest, needs emergency evaluation immediately - do not wait to see if
it improves, even if the cat still seems alert. Keep the cat as calm and undisturbed
as possible on the way to the vet, since stress and handling can worsen respiratory
distress; a covered carrier in a quiet space is often better than being held.
