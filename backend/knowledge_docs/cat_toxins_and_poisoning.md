# Common Cat Toxins and Poisoning

## Overview

Cats have limited liver enzymes for metabolizing many compounds that are safe or
only mildly risky for humans and dogs, which makes them uniquely sensitive to
several common household substances. The single most important cat-specific toxin
to know about is lilies (true lilies and daylilies, genus *Lilium* and
*Hemerocallis*): they are severely, often fatally, toxic to cats and are common in
bouquets and gardens, yet most people don't associate flowers with pet danger.

## Substances of Particular Concern for Cats

- **Lilies** - every part of the plant (petals, leaves, stem, pollen) and even the
  water in a vase are toxic. Ingesting a small amount, or simply grooming pollen off
  the fur, can cause acute kidney failure. This is a same-day emergency even before
  any symptoms appear.
- **Permethrin** - the active ingredient in many dog flea/tick spot-on treatments.
  Cats lack the liver enzyme to safely break it down; accidental application of a
  dog product to a cat (or a cat grooming a treated dog) is a well-documented and
  severe poisoning cause.
- **Onions and garlic** (including in cooked food, baby food, or broths) - damage
  red blood cells in cats.
- **Human medications** - even small amounts of common over-the-counter drugs like
  acetaminophen or ibuprofen are highly toxic to cats.
- **Chocolate** - toxic in a dose-dependent way, same general concern as in dogs.
- **Essential oils and liquid potpourri** - cats lack the liver pathway to
  metabolize many essential oil compounds; both ingestion and skin contact can cause
  toxicity.
- **Rat and mouse poison (rodenticides)** - direct ingestion, or eating a poisoned
  rodent, can both cause toxicity.
- **String, ribbon, and tinsel** - not a chemical toxin, but a common and serious
  cause of linear foreign body obstruction in cats who are drawn to play with them.

## Symptoms of Poisoning

- Drooling
- Vomiting
- Lethargy or wobbliness/incoordination
- Loss of appetite
- Tremors or seizures
- In lily/kidney-toxin cases: increased or decreased urination, which can progress
  to no urine output at all as kidney failure sets in

## Questions to Ask

- What exactly did the cat eat or get exposed to, and roughly how much?
- How long ago did this happen?
- Are there any lilies in the house - cut flowers, a garden, or even pollen that
  could have brushed onto fur?
- Was a flea/tick product recently applied - and was it labeled specifically for cats?
- Any vomiting, drooling, or wobbliness since the exposure?

## Typical Triage Level

**Emergency**, for any suspected or confirmed exposure to a known toxin - do not
wait for symptoms to appear before seeking care, especially for lily exposure, where
treatment started before kidney damage develops has a much better outcome than
treatment started afterward.

## Owner Guidance

Do not induce vomiting unless a vet or animal poison control specifically instructs
it - some substances cause more damage coming back up, and inducing vomiting
incorrectly can itself be dangerous. Bring the packaging, plant, or a photo of what
was ingested to the vet visit. If the exposure was a lily (any part, or pollen), treat
this as an emergency and go to a vet immediately even if the cat currently looks
completely normal.
