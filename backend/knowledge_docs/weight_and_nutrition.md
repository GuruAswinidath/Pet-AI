# Weight and Nutrition in Cats

## Overview

Maintaining a healthy weight is one of the highest-leverage things an owner can do
for a cat's long-term health - obesity in cats is strongly linked to diabetes,
joint problems, and a higher risk of the liver condition (hepatic lipidosis)
described in the "Loss of appetite" reference doc, since heavier cats mobilize fat
to the liver faster when they stop eating.

## Assessing Body Condition

Rather than a single "ideal weight" number (which varies enormously by breed,
frame size, and age), vets typically assess body condition by feel and appearance:

- Ribs should be easily felt with a light layer of fat, not visible, and not
  buried under a thick fat layer
- A visible waist should be noticeable when viewed from above
- The belly should not have a prominent, sagging fat pad when viewed from the side
  (some loose skin is normal and different from a fat pad)

A body condition assessment at a vet visit is more reliable than comparing to a
generic weight chart, since a large-frame cat and a small-frame cat can have very
different healthy weights.

## Feeding Guidelines

- Kittens need more frequent, calorie-dense meals to support rapid growth; adult
  cats generally do well on scheduled meals rather than free-feeding, which helps
  control weight and lets owners notice appetite changes early (an early warning
  sign for many conditions).
- Sudden diet changes are best introduced gradually over several days to avoid
  triggering GI upset (see the "Vomiting" and "Diarrhoea" reference docs).
- Fresh water should always be available; some cats drink more reliably from a
  moving water source (fountain-style bowls) than a still bowl.

## Common Questions Owners Ask

- "How do I know if my cat is overweight?" - A vet's body condition score at a
  routine visit is the most reliable check; visually, an absent waist and
  difficulty feeling the ribs are common signs.
- "Should I free-feed or schedule meals?" - Scheduled meals are generally easier
  for weight management and make it much easier to notice when a cat's appetite
  changes, which is clinically useful information.
- "Is it dangerous to put my cat on a diet?" - Weight loss in cats should be done
  gradually and under veterinary guidance - rapid, unsupervised calorie
  restriction in an overweight cat can itself trigger hepatic lipidosis, so "just
  feed less" isn't a safe DIY plan without guidance.

## When to See a Vet

Weight management itself is routine, non-urgent care best planned with a vet.
Treat it as more urgent if a cat's weight is dropping unintentionally (possible
underlying illness rather than diet) or if an overweight cat stops eating for more
than 24 hours - see the "Loss of appetite" reference doc for why that combination
is treated seriously in cats specifically.
