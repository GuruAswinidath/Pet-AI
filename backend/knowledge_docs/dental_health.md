# Dental Health in Cats

## Overview

Dental disease is one of the most common chronic conditions in adult and senior
cats, and one of the most under-recognized by owners - cats are good at hiding
mouth pain, and plaque/tartar buildup happens gradually enough that it's easy to
miss day to day. Left untreated, it progresses from mild gingivitis to periodontal
disease, tooth loss, and can become a source of chronic pain and, in advanced
cases, a route for infection to affect other organs.

## Common Conditions

- **Gingivitis** - inflamed, reddened gums, usually the earliest and most
  reversible stage.
- **Periodontal disease** - progression of untreated gingivitis into the deeper
  tissues supporting the teeth, which can lead to tooth loss and is not
  reversible, only manageable.
- **Tooth resorption** - a cat-specific condition where the tooth structure itself
  breaks down, often painful, and fairly common in adult and senior cats.
- **Stomatitis** - a more severe, widespread inflammatory condition of the mouth
  tissue, which can be significantly painful and harder to manage than routine
  dental disease.

## Symptoms

- Bad breath (a genuinely useful early clue, not just cosmetic)
- Visible tartar (yellow-brown buildup) on the teeth
- Red or bleeding gums
- Dropping food while eating, chewing on one side, or reluctance to eat hard food
- Pawing at the mouth or face
- Drooling, sometimes blood-tinged
- Reduced appetite or weight loss in more advanced cases

## Common Questions Owners Ask

- "My cat's breath smells bad - is that normal?" - Mild, non-offensive breath is
  common; genuinely foul or worsening breath usually indicates dental disease
  worth a vet check, not something to ignore as "just a cat thing."
- "Does my cat need a dental cleaning?" - Vets typically assess this via an oral
  exam (sometimes needing sedation for a full assessment) and recommend cleaning
  based on visible tartar and gum health, similar in concept to a human dental
  checkup.
- "Can dental disease affect my cat's overall health?" - Yes - chronic oral
  infection/inflammation is a source of ongoing pain and, in advanced cases, can
  contribute to systemic illness, which is part of why it's treated as more than
  a cosmetic issue.

## When to See a Vet

Routine dental disease is generally a "soon, not emergency" topic - schedule a
vet visit rather than watching and waiting once tartar, bad breath, or reluctance
to eat hard food is noticed. Escalate to same-day care if there's facial swelling,
significant bleeding, a cat has stopped eating entirely because of mouth pain, or
you see any of the red flags in the "Loss of appetite" reference doc alongside
dental symptoms.
