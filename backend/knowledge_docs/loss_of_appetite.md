# Loss of Appetite in Cats

## Overview

Cats are notably poor at going without food compared to most other pets. A cat that
stops eating - or eats only a small fraction of its normal intake - can begin
mobilizing fat to the liver faster than the liver can process it, leading to a
serious and potentially fatal condition called hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver
disease). This risk is highest in overweight cats, but it is not limited to them.
Loss of appetite is also frequently the first and only visible sign of a wide range
of underlying problems - dental pain, kidney disease, gastrointestinal disease,
infection, or stress - so it deserves attention even when the cat otherwise seems
"fine."

## Symptoms

- Skipping one or more meals
- Eating noticeably less than the usual amount
- Sniffing food and walking away
- Eating treats but refusing regular meals (can indicate dental/mouth pain rather
  than true lack of appetite)
- Weight loss over days to weeks if appetite loss is ongoing

## Questions to Ask

- Roughly how many hours or how many meals has it been since they last ate anything?
- Are they still drinking water normally?
- Is the cat overweight? Overweight cats are at higher risk of hepatic lipidosis
  from even short fasting periods.
- Any vomiting, diarrhea, hiding, or lethargy alongside the appetite change?
- Any recent stressors - new pet, moving house, boarding, a vet visit?
- Any bad breath, drooling, or pawing at the mouth (possible dental pain)?

## Red Flags

- Not eating at all for 2 or more days
- Not eating combined with vomiting or lethargy
- More than 24 hours without food in an overweight cat
- Not eating and hiding
- Yellowing of the gums, skin, or whites of the eyes (jaundice - a sign of advancing
  liver involvement)

## Typical Triage Level

**Soon to emergency**, depending on duration and accompanying signs. A single missed
meal in an otherwise normal cat is usually fine to watch. Anything past 24 hours,
especially in a heavier cat, or any appetite loss paired with other symptoms, should
not wait.

## Owner Guidance

Try offering a usual favorite or a strong-smelling warmed food to encourage eating,
and track exactly how much is actually consumed rather than just offered. Because
the liver risk in cats is faster-acting than in most other pets, don't apply a
"wait a few days and see" approach the way you might with a person who's not
hungry - by 48 hours without food, a vet visit is warranted even without any other
symptoms, and sooner if the cat is overweight or shows any other sign of illness.
